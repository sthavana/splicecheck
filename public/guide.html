The Ad Insertion Chain

How an ad gets into a stream, from the cue an encoder emits to the impression an ad server counts — and the dozen places along the way it quietly fails to.

01

Why putting an ad in a stream is hard

In broadcast, an ad break is a switch. In streaming, there is no switch — there are millions of independent players, each holding a slightly different piece of the timeline, each needing to be told what to play and when.

Linear television solved advertising with hardware. A playout automation system fires a relay at a scheduled moment, the router switches from programme to commercial, and every viewer watching that channel sees the same thing at the same instant. The break is a physical event in one building.

Streaming has none of those properties:

  • There is no single output. The same content leaves the packager as HLS and DASH, at six or eight bitrates, with several audio languages. Every one of those renditions has to be told about the break identically, or the ad plays on some devices and not others.
  • Viewers are not synchronised. One is at the live edge, one is thirty seconds behind on a slow connection, one is watching from the start of a DVR window. "Now" is different for each of them.
  • The ad is chosen per viewer. That is the entire commercial premise — addressable advertising is worth more than a broadcast spot precisely because it is different for each household. So the content cannot be baked in upstream.
  • Everything is best-effort HTTP. Segments are cached by CDNs, requested out of order, retried, abandoned. There is no guaranteed delivery moment to switch on.

So the industry split the problem in two. A signal travels with the content saying an ad opportunity begins here and lasts this long. Separately, a decision is made about what should fill it. The whole of what follows is the machinery that carries the first and executes the second — and almost every real-world failure lives at the joint between them.

02

The vocabulary, first

These words are used loosely in conversation and precisely in specifications. The precise meanings are what the systems actually implement.

TermWhat it actually means
AvailAn availability — a span of programme time the broadcaster has made available to be replaced by advertising. The opportunity, not the content.
BreakUsed interchangeably with avail in practice. Strictly, the break is the interruption as the viewer experiences it.
PodThe sequence of ads that fills one avail. A 90-second avail might hold a pod of three 30-second spots.
CreativeAn individual advert — the actual video file, plus its metadata and tracking URLs.
SlateFiller shown when the pod does not cover the avail. Often a station ident, a countdown, or black. Slate is the visible symptom of under-fill.
Fill rateThe proportion of available ad inventory actually filled with a paying creative. The number the commercial side cares about most.
CueThe signal marking the avail boundary. In practice, a SCTE-35 message.
Splice pointThe exact instant the switch should happen, expressed as a presentation timestamp.
Pre-rollTwo meanings. (1) An ad before the content starts. (2) The lead time between a cue arriving and the splice point it describes. Context distinguishes them; this page uses lead time for the second.
ManifestThe playlist a player reads to find segments: .m3u8 for HLS, .mpd for DASH.
ConditioningPreparing content so it can be spliced cleanly — an IDR frame at the boundary, segment durations that divide evenly, audio frames that align.
ImpressionA counted view of an ad. What is billed. Defined by measurement standards, not by whether a human looked at it.
03

The chain, end to end

Before the detail, the shape. A cue is born in a control room, survives several format changes, and is finally acted on either by a server or by the player itself.

  1. Playout automation decidesA scheduling system knows the break is coming. It emits SCTE-104 over ethernet to the encoder, or embeds it in the SDI ancillary data.
  2. The encoder translatesSCTE-104 becomes SCTE-35, a binary section carried in the compressed transport stream on its own PID, timed against the same clock as the video.
  3. The packager transcribesThe packager reads the SCTE-35 and writes it into the manifests it produces — as HLS tags, DASH periods, or event streams. This is a translation, and translations can be wrong.
  4. The CDN distributesManifests and segments are cached and served. The CDN has no idea any of this is about advertising.
  5. Someone acts on itEither a server-side service rewrites the manifest with ad segments in it, or the player reads the markers and handles the break itself.
  6. Impressions are countedTracking beacons fire at start, 25%, 50%, 75% and completion. Whoever played the ad is responsible for firing them.
The joint that matters

Step 3 is where most real faults originate and where almost nobody looks. The encoder's cue and the packager's tag are two different artefacts describing the same event, produced by different vendors' software. Nothing in the normal delivery path compares them.

04

SCTE-35, in detail

ANSI/SCTE 35 defines a single binary structure, the splice_info_section, that says something is about to happen on this stream at this timestamp. Everything else about ad insertion is built on it.

A section is typically 20–200 bytes. It carries a command, an optional list of descriptors, and a CRC. Here is one, decoded:

# base64: /DAvAAAAAAAA///wFAVIAACPf+/+c2nALv4AUsz1AAAAAAAKAAhDVUVJAAABNWLbowo=

table_id            0xFC
section_length      47
protocol_version    0
pts_adjustment      0          ← added to every timestamp below
tier                0xFFF
splice_command      splice_insert

  splice_event_id       0x4800008F
  out_of_network        true       ← leaving the programme
  program_splice        true
  splice_time.pts_time  1936310318 ← 90kHz ticks = 21514.6s
  break_duration        60.294s
  auto_return           true       ← come back automatically
  unique_program_id     0
  avail_num / expected  0 / 0

descriptors         1 × avail_descriptor (CUEI)
CRC_32              valid

Two commands do nearly all the work

splice_insert

The older, simpler form, inherited from cable. It says directly: leave the network now (or at this PTS), for this long, and optionally come back by yourself.

out_of_network distinguishes the departure from the return. auto_return means no closing signal will be sent — the duration is the whole contract.

time_signal + segmentation_descriptor

The modern form. time_signal carries only a timestamp; all meaning lives in the descriptors attached to it.

A segmentation_descriptor says what kind of boundary this is (0x30 Provider Advertisement Start, 0x31 its End, 0x34/0x35 Placement Opportunity, 0x10/0x11 Programme Start and End) and carries an identifier for the segment.

The fields that cause real trouble

FieldWhy it matters in practice
pts_timeThe splice point on the 90kHz presentation clock. 33 bits, so it wraps every 26.5 hours — code that forgets this breaks roughly once a day.
pts_adjustmentAdded to every timestamp in the section. Non-zero when a device has re-timed the stream. Ignoring it puts every break in the wrong place.
splice_immediate_flag"Switch now, no timestamp." Leaves the ad decision server no time to fetch creatives; a common cause of slate on the first seconds of a break.
segmentation_upidIdentifies what is being segmented. Types include Ad-ID, EIDR, UUID, and MPU — a vendor-defined blob that in practice often carries JSON with pod position and asset identifiers.
segmentation_duration40 bits at 90kHz. Where present, it defines the avail length without needing a closing descriptor.
CRC_32MPEG-2 style, over the whole section. Strict decisioning rejects a bad CRC outright — and a bad CRC is usually invisible, because most tools never check it.
tierLets a downstream device filter sections not intended for it. Rarely used, occasionally the reason a cue appears to vanish.
Lead time

The most consequential property of a cue is not in the spec at all: how far ahead of the splice point it arrives. An ad decision server needs to call out, select a pod, and have creatives transcoded and ready. Under about four seconds of lead time, fill rates fall sharply. Four to eight seconds is the usual target, and encoders are configured to emit cues that far in advance.

05

How the cue travels

The same 35 bytes are carried five different ways depending on where in the chain you look. Knowing which carriage you are reading is most of knowing what can go wrong.

In the media

Transport stream, own PID. The PMT declares an elementary stream of stream_type 0x86, and splice_info_sections arrive on that PID. This is the contribution and broadcast form — the signal as it leaves the encoder.

ID3 PRIV frame in a metadata PES. Despite the above, most HLS transport streams carry the cue this way instead: a stream_type 0x15 metadata stream whose PES packets contain an ID3 tag, with a PRIV frame owned by urn:scte:scte35:2013:bin wrapping the raw section. The PES header's PTS places it on the clock. A tool that only looks for 0x86 will report "no inband signalling" on a stream that is full of it.

emsg box. In CMAF and DASH, an Event Message Box sits at the top level of a segment, ahead of the moof. Version 0 is timed relative to the segment; version 1 carries an absolute media time. The MPD advertises this with <InbandEventStream>.

In the manifest

HLS. There are three conventions and streams often use two at once:

# the modern form — carries the section, base64 or hex
#EXT-X-DATERANGE:ID="b1",START-DATE="2026-09-18T10:00:12.000Z",
                 PLANNED-DURATION=30.0,SCTE35-OUT=0xFC3020...

# the widely-implemented form — just brackets the break
#EXT-X-CUE-OUT:30.000
#EXT-X-DISCONTINUITY
... ad segments ...
#EXT-X-CUE-IN

# vendor forms still in the wild
#EXT-OATCLS-SCTE35:/DAvAAAA...
#EXT-X-SCTE35:CUE="/DAvAAAA..."

DASH. Two quite different models. Either a Period-level <EventStream> carries events with a presentationTime and duration, or — more commonly for stitched output — each avail becomes its own Period. The multi-period model is structurally cleaner but introduces its own class of problem: period continuity, presentation time offsets, and whether the ad period presents the same representations as the content around it.

HLS interstitials

A newer, different model: EXT-X-DATERANGE with CLASS="com.apple.hls.interstitial" names an asset (X-ASSET-URI) or an asset list for the player to fetch and play. Nothing is spliced into the playlist at all. It is closer to client-side insertion wearing manifest clothing, and it is measured differently — duration and fill depend on what the asset list returns at playback.

06

Client-side ad insertion

The player does the work. It sees the break coming, pauses the content, asks an ad server what to play, fetches the creative, plays it, fires the tracking, and resumes.

What actually happens

  1. The player, or an SDK inside it, reads the ad markers — or is given a schedule up front via VMAP.
  2. At the break, it issues an ad request to the ad decision server: a URL carrying device type, consent state, content identifiers, and targeting parameters.
  3. The server returns VAST — an XML document naming one or more creatives, their durations, their media file URLs at several bitrates, and a list of tracking URLs.
  4. The SDK loads the creative into a second video element, hides the content element, and plays.
  5. It fires tracking beacons at start, first quartile, midpoint, third quartile and complete.
  6. It swaps back and seeks the content element to the return point.

What it is good at

  • The player knows everything about the viewer — screen size, volume, viewability, consent — so targeting and verification are rich.
  • Interactive formats work: clickthrough, overlays, skippable ads with a countdown.
  • No transcoding of the ad is required. The creative plays as delivered.
  • Content delivery is untouched, so a failure in the ad path cannot break playback.

What goes wrong

  • Ad blockers. The ad request goes to a recognisable third-party host from the client. It is trivially blocked, and widely is.
  • Buffering at the switch. Two video elements, two decoders, two connections. The transition is visible on weak devices and slow networks.
  • SDK availability. Smart TVs, set-top boxes and games consoles each need a working SDK. Coverage is never complete.
  • Latency. The ad request happens at the break, so its round trip is in the critical path.
07

Server-side ad insertion

A service between the origin and the player rewrites the manifest so the ad segments sit in it as though they were always part of the programme. The player just plays a stream.

What actually happens

  1. The player requests a manifest from the stitcher, not the origin. It receives a session-specific URL — every viewer gets their own.
  2. The stitcher polls the origin manifest and watches for cues.
  3. When an avail approaches, it calls the ad decision server with that session's targeting data and receives a pod.
  4. It rewrites the manifest: the ad's segments are listed inline, bracketed by discontinuity tags, in place of the programme segments the avail covers.
  5. The player fetches and plays those segments like any others.
  6. The stitcher fires the tracking beacons, inferring progress from which segments were requested.

Conditioning: the unglamorous prerequisite

For the splice to be invisible, the ad has to be encoded compatibly with the content: same codec and profile, same resolution ladder, same audio configuration, segment durations that divide the avail evenly, and an IDR frame at every segment boundary. Ads arrive from agencies as single MP4 files at arbitrary settings, so an SSAI platform runs a conditioning pipeline that transcodes and segments every creative to match every publisher's ladder, in advance.

This is most of the operational cost of running SSAI, and when a break plays badly the cause is frequently here rather than in the signalling.

What it is good at

  • Ad blockers cannot distinguish ad segments from content segments — they come from the same host, in the same format, on the same connection.
  • No SDK required. Anything that can play the stream can play the ads, which matters enormously for long-tail smart TVs.
  • Seamless playback: one decoder, one buffer, no switch.
  • The ad request happens ahead of the break, off the critical path.

What goes wrong

  • Under-fill. The pod does not cover the avail and the tail is slate or black. Directly measurable lost revenue.
  • Passthrough. The break is opened and filled with the programme itself. The manifest is well-formed, the player is happy, and no ad was delivered.
  • Missing discontinuities. Substituted content without a discontinuity tag makes players glitch, stall or drop audio at the splice.
  • Weaker measurement. The server infers viewing from segment requests. It cannot see whether the player was muted, backgrounded or off-screen.
  • Cache efficiency. Per-session manifests are unique, so manifest caching is lost (segments still cache).
08

Remote Periods, and the hole in the timeline

In multi-period DASH the ad break is its own Period — and the packager often does not have the ad when it writes the manifest. So it writes the hole instead, and names the service that will fill it.

That is what xlink:href on a Period means. The packager reserves the extent, states where the content will come from, and moves on. Something resolves it later: the player itself, a CDN, or a stitcher sitting in front of both.

<Period id="content-1" start="PT0S" duration="PT120S">
  <AdaptationSet mimeType="video/mp4"> ... </AdaptationSet>
</Period>

<Period id="avail-1" start="PT120S" duration="PT90S"
        xlink:href="https://ads.example/decide?pod=1"
        xlink:actuate="onRequest">
  <EventStream schemeIdUri="urn:scte:scte35:2014:xml+bin"> ... </EventStream>
</Period>

<Period id="content-2" start="PT210S" duration="PT120S">
  <AdaptationSet mimeType="video/mp4"> ... </AdaptationSet>
</Period>

The middle Period has no AdaptationSet at all. It has a start, a duration, usually the SCTE-35 that describes the avail, and an address. Everything a player needs to lay out the timeline is there; everything it needs to play the break is somewhere else.

onLoad and onRequest

xlink:actuate says when the resolution happens, and the two values buy opposite things.

ValueResolvesWhat it costs
onLoad Whenever the manifest is parsed On a live MPD refreshed every few seconds, every viewer re-requests the decision at that rate. The answer can change under a player that is already buffering.
onRequest When playback reaches the Period The decision happens at the splice point, so its latency is playback latency. A slow answer is a stall the viewer sees.

The attribute is optional and defaults to onRequest, but implementations disagreed about it for long enough that leaving it out is a portability risk rather than a shorthand. Stating it is free.

Lead time, again

The lead-time problem from the cue section returns here in a sharper form. With onRequest there is no lead time at all by construction: the request goes out at the moment the ad is needed. Whatever the decision service takes — call-out, auction, creative selection — the player is waiting for it. This is the single largest reason remote Periods under-fill in production, and it is invisible in the manifest.

What goes wrong

  • No duration on the placeholder. Without @duration nothing knows how long the avail is until the remote document has been fetched. A live client cannot place the live edge past it, and the decision service cannot be told how much inventory to fill.
  • The service does not answer. A timeout or a 5xx leaves the player with nothing to play at that point: a stall, a skip, or a jump to the content after the break, depending on the client. The avail is lost and the manifest never said so.
  • It answers with the wrong length. Every Period after the placeholder is laid out against the declared duration. Resolve 60s into a 90s hole and the timeline shifts by 30s — the break returns early and the gap shows as a freeze. Resolve 120s and the content after it is cut.
  • It answers with a different encode. Resolved content in another codec, resolution or frame rate forces a decoder tear-down at both ends of the break. This is the black frame that gets reported as "the ad broke the stream" — and it is the ad service's encode, not the packager's, that caused it.
  • It answers with nothing. An empty document is how an unfilled avail is supposed to look: the break collapses and content resumes. Nothing is broken. It is also inventory that went unsold, and it is indistinguishable from success unless someone is counting.
Why this one is different

Every other fault in this guide can be found by reading a manifest. This one cannot. The text is well-formed either way — the difference between an avail that plays and an avail that is lost is the response to a request that has not been made yet, to a service that is not yours, at a moment you do not control. The only way to check it is to make the call yourself and see what comes back.

SpliceCheck reads the placeholder attributes from the manifest, and will optionally resolve the remote Periods — making the request a player would make — to report whether the service answers, how long it takes against the playback deadline, whether the returned duration matches the hole reserved for it, and whether the ad is encoded like the programme around it.

09

DAI, and why the terms blur

Dynamic Ad Insertion is the umbrella: any system that decides at playback time what ad a given viewer sees, rather than baking it in. Both CSAI and SSAI are DAI.

The term is muddied because Google's product is literally called DAI, and it is a server-side product — so in many conversations "DAI" is used to mean SSAI specifically. When someone says DAI, it is worth establishing which they mean.

The distinction that actually matters is not client versus server. It is who talks to the ad decision server, and who is responsible for counting the impression. Everything else follows from that.

Hybrid, in practice

Most large deployments are not pure. Common combinations:

  • Server-side stitching with client-side tracking. The stitcher splices the ads but passes the tracking URLs to the player over a side-channel, so beacons fire from the client with real viewability data. This is the most common serious configuration, and it is what SCTE-130 and the IAB's server-side guidance were written to support.
  • Server-side for live, client-side for VOD. Live needs the robustness; VOD can afford the richer client formats.
  • Interstitials. HLS interstitials are a third model: the manifest names an asset and the player fetches it. Server-signalled, client-executed.

10

Ad decisioning

The signalling says an avail exists. Decisioning decides what goes in it — and that is a separate industry with its own protocols.

VAST, VMAP and the response

VAST (Video Ad Serving Template) is the response format: XML describing one creative — its duration, its media files at several bitrates and codecs, its clickthrough, and its tracking URLs. A VAST wrapper points at another VAST document on another server, which is how ad networks chain; each hop adds latency and a chance of failure.

VMAP (Video Multiple Ad Playlist) describes the schedule — where the breaks are in a piece of content and what kind of ad each takes. It is used mainly for VOD, where the break positions are known in advance. Live uses the in-stream cues instead.

<!-- VAST, reduced to the parts that matter -->
<Ad id="1187">
  <InLine>
    <Creatives><Creative><Linear>
      <Duration>00:00:30</Duration>
      <MediaFiles>
        <MediaFile bitrate="2000" width="1920" height="1080"
                   type="video/mp4">https://…/creative-1080.mp4</MediaFile>
      </MediaFiles>
      <TrackingEvents>
        <Tracking event="start">…</Tracking>
        <Tracking event="firstQuartile">…</Tracking>
        <Tracking event="midpoint">…</Tracking>
        <Tracking event="thirdQuartile">…</Tracking>
        <Tracking event="complete">…</Tracking>
      </TrackingEvents>
    </Linear></Creative></Creatives>
  </InLine>
</Ad>

Building a pod

A 90-second avail is not one 90-second ad. The decision server assembles a pod under constraints that are commercial as much as technical: separation rules (no two car advertisers adjacent), frequency capping per viewer, priority between guaranteed and programmatic demand, and the simple arithmetic of fitting 15s and 30s spots into the exact avail length.

When the arithmetic does not work out, the remainder is filled with house ads, a promo, or slate. That gap is what a fill-rate metric is measuring.

Where the money is decided

Demand reaches the decision server through direct-sold campaigns, programmatic exchanges, or a waterfall of both. In live streaming the whole decision — request, auction, pod assembly, response — has to complete inside the lead time the cue provided. This is why the four-second figure in §04 is not a technicality: it is the budget the entire commercial stack has to fit inside.

11

Low latency, and the decision budget

Cutting the distance to live from thirty seconds to two does not change what ad insertion has to do. It changes how long there is to do it — and that turns out to be the constraint everything else bends around.

An ordinary HLS player sits three target durations behind the live edge: with six-second segments, about eighteen seconds. A DASH player sits at suggestedPresentationDelay, typically a similar figure. That distance is not waste; it is the buffer that absorbs a slow request, a rebuffer, a bitrate switch. It is also the window in which an ad decision can happen without anybody noticing.

Low latency spends it. Both protocols get there the same way — publish the media before it is finished — and both then need a contract telling clients how to consume something incomplete.

The two contracts

LL-HLSLL-DASH

Both contracts can be stated incorrectly, and the failure is rarely a stream that does not play. It is a stream that plays badly in a way that looks like a network problem: a PART-HOLD-BACK under three part durations puts clients closer to live than the packager can sustain, so they run out of parts and rebuffer. Chunked availability without availabilityTimeComplete="false" tells clients a segment is finished when it is not, so they request it and get a truncation or a 404.

The decision budget

Here is the number that decides whether a low-latency stream can carry advertising at all. PART-HOLD-BACK is typically about one second. Latency@target is typically two to three. That is the entire time available, from the cue appearing to the splice happening, for an ad decision service to call out, run an auction, select a pod, confirm the creatives are transcoded and have them ready to stitch.

At eighteen seconds of latency, none of that was urgent. At one second, a decision that takes four hundred milliseconds is using nearly half the budget — and a decision that misses it does not arrive late, it does not arrive at all: the splice point passes and the avail goes unfilled.

Lead time is the answer, and it is not in any tag

The way out is not a faster ad server. It is to stop making the decision at the splice point. A cue carried far enough ahead of the break gives the decision the time it needs regardless of how close to live the viewer is sitting — which is why EXT-X-DATERANGE with a future START-DATE, and a DASH Event with a presentationTime ahead of the live edge, matter far more at low latency than they ever did at eighteen seconds.

No tag states the lead time. It is the gap between when the cue first appears in the manifest and when the splice happens, and reading it requires watching the manifest over time rather than looking at one copy of it.

The trap that only bites advertising

Delta updates and DATERANGE

EXT-X-SERVER-CONTROL:CAN-SKIP-UNTIL offers clients a cheaper playlist: a delta update with the older segments omitted. On a low-latency stream, where the playlist is refetched several times a second, most clients take it.

Unless the packager also sets CAN-SKIP-DATERANGES=YES, those delta updates drop the EXT-X-DATERANGE tags along with the skipped segments. Clients using delta updates stop seeing the ad signalling; clients doing full reloads still see it. The break exists for some viewers and not others — and every manifest involved is valid, so nothing upstream reports a fault. The only symptom is a fill rate that is inexplicably lower than the number of avails signalled.

What to check

  • The hold-back against the part target. Below three parts, the client cannot keep its buffer fed. This is the most common reason a low-latency stream is unwatchable rather than merely late.
  • Blocking reload actually enabled. Parts without CAN-BLOCK-RELOAD=YES mean the client polls, and the latency the parts bought is spent again waiting for the next poll.
  • One live point, not two. A DASH manifest that states both suggestedPresentationDelay and a Latency@target several seconds apart has two populations of players watching different moments, and a cue reaches them at different times.
  • A target the segmentation can reach. Asking for three seconds with six-second segments and no chunked delivery is asking for something arithmetically impossible.
  • Delta updates that keep the signalling. Above.

SpliceCheck checks each of these in both protocols, and reports the decision budget explicitly — the hold-back or the latency target — because it is the figure most teams have never actually written down.

12

Measurement, and why it is contested

An impression is what gets billed, so the definition of one is a commercial instrument, not just a technical event.

The standard progression fires five beacons: start, firstQuartile, midpoint, thirdQuartile, complete. Billing usually keys on start or on a viewability threshold; completion rate is a quality metric that buyers scrutinise.

Client-side tracking

The player knows whether the video element was visible, what proportion of it was on screen, and whether audio was muted. The IAB's viewability standard for video — 50% of pixels for two continuous seconds — is only measurable from the client, and OMID (Open Measurement) exists to let third-party verification vendors observe this inside the player.

Server-side tracking

The stitcher infers playback from which segments were requested. This is robust against blocking and works on devices with no SDK, but it cannot see the screen. A segment request proves bytes were delivered, not that anyone was watching — and a player that buffers ahead then abandons will look like a completed view unless the stitcher is careful.

Why hybrids exist

Server-side delivery with client-side tracking gets the robustness of SSAI and the measurement quality of CSAI, at the cost of a side-channel between them. The complexity is real, which is why plenty of deployments accept server-side tracking and the discount that comes with it.

There is also the adversarial dimension. SIVT (Sophisticated Invalid Traffic) — bots, spoofed devices, server farms requesting segments — is a persistent tax, and server-side tracking is more exposed to it because there is less signal available to distinguish a real player from a script requesting segments on schedule.

The hard part

None of these raise an error anywhere. The CDN serves 200s, the player reports no errors, QoE dashboards stay green. The only signals are commercial — fill rate drifting down, impressions not reconciling — and by the time someone notices, the evidence has rolled out of the DVR window.

Which is why the tooling that matters here is not player monitoring. It is something that reads the signalling itself, continuously, and knows the difference between a real fault and a live window behaving normally.

Glossary

ADS
Ad Decision Server. The system that chooses what plays in an avail.
Beacon
A tracking URL fired to record that something happened — usually a 1×1 pixel request.
CSAI
Client-Side Ad Insertion. The player fetches and plays the ad.
DAI
Dynamic Ad Insertion. The umbrella term; often used to mean SSAI specifically.
Discontinuity
A manifest marker telling the player that timing and encoding parameters change at this point.
emsg
Event Message Box. Carries events, including SCTE-35, inside a CMAF or DASH segment.
GOP
Group of Pictures. The span between keyframes; splices must land on one.
IDR frame
A keyframe that fully resets decoding. A splice point must be one.
PCR
Program Clock Reference. The transport stream's own clock, which leads presentation time by the decoder buffer delay.
PID
Packet Identifier. Identifies an elementary stream inside a transport stream.
PMT
Program Map Table. Declares which PIDs carry what, including the SCTE-35 stream.
PTS
Presentation Timestamp. When a frame should be shown, on a 90 kHz clock.
SSAI
Server-Side Ad Insertion. Ads are spliced into the manifest before the player sees it.
Stitcher
The service performing SSAI manifest manipulation.
UPID
Unique Program Identifier. Identifies the content a segmentation descriptor refers to.
VAST
The XML ad response naming creatives and tracking URLs.
VMAP
The XML ad schedule saying where breaks fall in a piece of content.
Waterfall
Trying demand sources in priority order until one fills.

Written for engineers arriving at streaming advertising from either side — video delivery people meeting ad tech, or ad tech people meeting video. Terminology follows the specifications rather than vendor naming, which is where most confusion in this subject actually comes from.
