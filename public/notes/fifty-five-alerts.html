Fifty-five alerts and nothing wrong

Siva Thavanathan 18 September 2026

I built a monitor for the ad signalling in live video streams. It polls a stream, works out where the ad breaks are, and raises an alert when something changes for the worse.

I pointed it at two live streams that were working perfectly well. Ninety minutes later it had raised 55 alerts. Not one described a real fault.

I could have filtered them, or raised the thresholds until the noise stopped. Instead I went through all 55 and asked, each time: why is the stream allowed to look like this?

There turned out to be seven distinct answers. They are all specific to video, but the shape of the problem is not.

What the monitor watches

Enough background to follow the rest.

A live stream is delivered as a manifest — a text file listing the last few minutes of video in short segments. The player refetches it every few seconds. Old segments fall off the front as new ones are added, so the manifest describes a sliding window over the stream, typically a few minutes wide.

Ad breaks are announced in that manifest with markers called SCTE-35. A break, or avail, is bracketed by a cue out and a cue in. Downstream, an ad insertion service reads those markers and substitutes commercials into the break.

A stream is also delivered at several renditions — the same content at 720p, 480p, audio-only — and every one of them must carry identical ad signalling. If they disagree, whether the ad plays depends on which rendition the player happened to pick.

The one design decision that matters for this story: alerts fire on transitions, not on state. A fault alerts when it appears, stays quiet while it persists, and alerts once when it clears. A monitor that pages you every minute about something that has been true since Tuesday is a monitor people mute.

The first long run

I left it polling two streams every twenty seconds: one HLS with four renditions, one multi-period DASH. Both were public demo services, both healthy throughout.

The first two lines are the tell. VERDICT_DEGRADED and ERRORS_CLEARED traded places ten times each. The monitor was deciding the stream had broken, then that it had recovered, roughly every ten minutes, for an hour and a half.

Nothing about the streams changed in that time. Ads played. Players were happy.

The tempting reading is that live streams are noisy and a monitor needs a tolerance band. That reading is wrong, and it is worth being precise about why. Every one of these alerts was the monitor asserting something false about the stream. Widening a threshold does not make a false assertion true — it just makes the tool assert it less often, while blinding it to the real fault that looks identical.

Seven reasons a healthy stream looks broken

1. One fault, counted four times

A stream with four renditions raises the same finding four times, once per rendition. My alerting turned each into its own alert.

Not subtle, and not interesting — but it inflated the count enough to obscure everything else. One fault is one alert; the rendition count belongs in the detail line.

2. The window opens part-way through a break

The manifest is a sliding window. Sometimes it begins in the middle of an ad break, so the first marker in it is a cue in — a return from a break whose departure has already aged off the front.

My parser saw a return with nothing to return from and called it an orphan: signal lost between encoder and packager. It is nothing of the sort. It is the window sliding, and it happens to every live stream a few times an hour.

The fix is not to stop reporting orphans. A cue in with no cue out, after a break that paired correctly, is a real fault — that one is not the window boundary. So: the leading one is expected, any later one is not.

3. The next break then looks nested

Straight after that. If the window opened inside a break, that break never closes within the window. The next cue out then arrives while one is still open, which reads as a nested break — invalid, and a genuine cause of wedged ad-insertion state machines.

Except it is the same artifact wearing a different hat. The previous break was clipped by the window, so of course nothing closed it.

Taken together, 2 and 3 are what made the stream flap between pass and fail every ten minutes: the window crossing a break boundary, twice per cycle.

4. A tag left behind by the window

This one I enjoyed. A DATERANGE tag — which declares its own start time — stays in the manifest until its whole range has rolled out. So the oldest one ends up sitting in front of a later segment than the one it originally preceded.

I had just added a rule comparing a tag's declared time against the position it occupies. It fired within minutes on a healthy stream, reporting a 1.92s disagreement.

The tell was that the number grew. Next poll it was 24.96s. A real inconsistency is a constant; a drift that widens on every refresh is the window moving underneath something that is standing still.

5. Renditions roll independently

Cross-rendition comparison is the check I care most about, because renditions disagreeing is what produces “the ad played on Roku but not on iOS”.

But the renditions are separate files, fetched at slightly different moments, each with its own window. Near the start of the shared window, one may have already trimmed a break's cue out while another still carries it. The break then exists in one and not the other, for no reason anyone did anything wrong.

So the comparison holds back from the boundary by a couple of segment durations. A genuinely missing break is still caught on the next poll, once it has moved away from the edge.

6. The same thing at the other end

I fixed 5, shipped it, and it came back — at the opposite boundary.

Audio segments are shorter than video segments, so an audio rendition reaches the return from a break sooner than the video ones. At the live edge the newest break is therefore closed in audio and still open in video. Only closed breaks are comparable, so the counts differed: audio 5, video 4.

The same class of artifact, at the end of the window instead of the start. I should have seen it when I wrote the first fix, and did not.

7. The newest period is still being written

On the DASH stream, a check comparing audio and video durations within a period reported a 2-second mismatch.

Audio and video are published independently. The newest period on a live manifest is still filling, so its tracks legitimately differ by a segment or more until it closes. Measured on a completed period the same figure is tens of milliseconds.

The check is worth having — accumulated A/V skew is real lip-sync drift. It just cannot be measured on a period that has not finished.

Suppress at the boundary, keep the rule everywhere else

Every one of the seven has the same shape. The rule was right. The stream was right. They disagreed only where the observation window cuts across something continuous.

So none of the fixes weakened a rule. Each one identified the edge and excluded it:

  • A cue in with no cue out is expected at the top of the window, and an error anywhere after a break that paired correctly.
  • A break near either end of the shared window is skipped for cross-rendition comparison. One in the middle is not.
  • A tag whose declared time drifts earlier as the window slides is the window moving. One declaring a time later than it sits is a real inconsistency, and still reports.
  • A/V skew is not measured on the period still being written. It is measured on every completed one.

The distinction that matters: a threshold makes the tool blunter everywhere. Excluding the boundary makes it quieter in exactly the place the observation is meaningless, and no quieter anywhere else.

That is harder than it sounds, because it requires knowing why the stream looks that way. You cannot do it from the spec. You have to watch the thing run and be willing to treat your own tool as the suspect.

The one that nearly went the other way

A false positive that announces itself as absurd is easy. The dangerous one is plausible.

Later I added a check that reads the ad markers carried inside the media segments themselves and compares them with what the manifest claims. The manifest is only a transcription: an encoder emits the cue into the stream, a packager writes a tag describing it, and the two can disagree.

The first run against a live service gave this:

BreakManifestFrom the segmentsDrift
113:39:56.16013:39:56.2850.125s
213:41:57.12013:41:57.2450.125s
313:43:58.08013:43:58.2050.125s
413:45:59.04013:45:59.1650.125s
513:48:00.00013:48:00.1250.125s

125 milliseconds, consistently. That is an entirely credible packager defect. It is about four frames. I could have written it up.

The giveaway is the consistency. A real transcription error drifts, or varies with content. A constant offset across five independent breaks is a systematic error in the measurement.

And it was. To place a cue on the wall clock you need an anchor in the segment. I had used the PCR, the stream's clock reference. But the timestamp a manifest's dates refer to is the first presentation timestamp, and the PCR leads it by the decoder buffer delay.

Anchored correctly, all five breaks come out at 0.000s. The manifest and the stream agree exactly.

A tool that reports a 125ms fault that does not exist is worse than no tool, because someone will go looking for it.

After

I cleared the history and left it running again on the same two streams.

Polls222 across 66 minutes
Failed fetches0
Polls reporting errors0
Alerts0

Not one alert, while both windows slid continuously, breaks rolled in and out, and one stream's period count moved between 13 and 25.

The rules are all still there. Every one of the seven conditions still reports when it occurs away from a boundary, and there are tests for both halves of each: the case that must fire, and the case that must stay silent.

What I would take from it

Alert fatigue does not show up in a unit test. My test suite passed throughout. Every one of these needed an hour of real traffic to appear, because each depends on where a moving window happens to fall.

A monitor that cries wolf is worse than no monitor. Not equivalent — worse. It trains its reader to dismiss it, and the dismissal habit does not distinguish between the false alerts and the real one.

Suspect your own tool first. Every one of these alerts was, on its face, a plausible fault in someone else's system. The instinct to believe your own output is the thing to fight.

A number that is suspiciously consistent is measuring you. Real faults vary. A constant is almost always your instrument.

The code is at github.com/sthavana/splicecheck, with the seven fixes and their tests. The commit messages say what each one got wrong, which is the part I would want to read. The tool itself is here, and how ad insertion works covers the domain this sits in.
